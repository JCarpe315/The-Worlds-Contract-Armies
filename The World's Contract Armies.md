# PART I — WHAT THIS INDUSTRY IS

## 1.1 The legal trap: why "mercenary" is a word that almost never works

The modern law of mercenaries rests on **Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions, Article 47 (1977)**. It defines a mercenary through six cumulative conditions: specially recruited for the fight; takes direct part; motivated by private gain with pay substantially above the party's own soldiers; not a national of any party; not a member of any party's armed forces; and not sent by a state as part of its armed forces. Legal scholars call the definition "unattainable" — by design it excludes almost everyone who actually does the work [S: ICRC, Geneva Academy].

On top of this: the **1989 UN International Convention against the Recruitment, Use, Financing and Training of Mercenaries** (in force 2001) criminalizes the trade but attracted only about three dozen state parties. **Mercenary activity is not a crime under the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court.** The 1977 OAU Convention covers Africa. The **Montreux Document (2008)** — now supported by 61 states and three international organizations — and the **International Code of Conduct Association (ICoCA, 2013)** acknowledge the gap and try to govern what they cannot prohibit. Their own text concedes that private military company employees "sometimes meet the conditions" of mercenary status but that the Article 47 definition "excludes most" of them [S: Swiss FDFA, ICoCA].

The consequences of this architecture are not incidental; they are the business model. A firm can be lawful while doing things that would be war crimes if the same men wore a different uniform — if it sits inside the gap. The ways to sit inside the gap, documented across this file, are: have the client state confer military ranks (the UAE did this for an American hit team); be licensed by the client's state (Syria's 2013 decree); sign contracts with the client's defense ministry (Russia's 2023 decree absorbed every volunteer formation); hold a "defensive-only" charter; or be formally absorbed into a national guard (Russia, February 2024).

**Two modern legal wedges bypass the trap, and both matter for where this goes:** the human-trafficking frame (Amnesty International adopted it in September 2026 — recruitment by deception, document confiscation, and coerced service is prosecutable as trafficking under the UNTOC Palermo Protocol, no mercenary definition required) [S: Amnesty, 10 Sep 2026]; and state attribution under **ARSIWA Articles 5 and 8** (the Articles on State Responsibility — conduct of an entity empowered to exercise governmental authority, or directed or controlled by a state, is attributable to that state), available since 2001 and, as of this writing, used in zero prosecutions [S/NA: US Naval War College, International Law Studies].

# PART II — THE ANCESTORS: FIVE CENTURIES OF RENTED FORCE

To understand the modern industry it helps to know that it is not modern. Every feature of the twenty-first-century contract army has a documented ancestor.

## 2.1 The Vatican's foreign legions (the industry's original client)

The oldest continuously operating foreign-contracted bodyguard corps in the world belongs to the **Pontifical Swiss Guard**, founded 22 January 1506 — a sovereign renting a foreign military unit, unbroken, for over five centuries. After the 1981 attempt on John Paul II's life, the Guard modernized and now provides plain-clothes protection on every papal journey [A: Swiss Guard records].

More instructive still is the **Papal Zouaves (1860–1870)** — the closest historical analog to the modern private military company. Pius IX's 1860 appeal called Catholics worldwide to defend the Papal States; the corps was organized by a priest who was a former Belgian army officer and a Swiss veteran of the Papal Guard, and by 1868 it numbered **4,592 volunteers from roughly thirty countries** — Dutch, French, Belgians, Canadians, an Irish "St. Patrick Battalion," even a sailor from the South Seas. They wore the pope's colors, called themselves the "Ninth Crusade," fought at Castelfidardo and Mentana, suppressed brigandage, nursed cholera victims — and when Rome fell in September 1870, **760 survivors were re-flagged within weeks as France's Légion de Volontaires de l'Ouest** and sent against the Prussians, where 216 of 300 died covering a retreat at Loigny. The pattern this document tracks — foreign volunteers under an ideological wrapper, instantly redeployed under a new client's flag when the original client collapses — is 156 years old [S/A: EBSCO, Wikipedia].

One member of the Zouaves is worth naming for American readers: **John Surratt**, a fugitive accused in the Lincoln assassination, joined the corps under an alias while hiding from US federal agents [B: encyclopedia.com].

## 2.2 The condottieri and the dogs of war

Renaissance Italy's **condottieri** — contract captains paid by city-states — professionalized war and made it a market; the Swiss pikemen sold themselves across Europe; the French Foreign Legion (1831) made foreign soldiery a permanent state institution rather than a company. The Congo Crisis of the 1960s produced the modern template: **Mike Hoare's "5 Commando"** and Bob Denard's white mercenary companies fighting for secessionist Katanga, followed by Denard's remarkable career in the Comoros Islands — **four coups between 1975 and 1995**, after which he ran the archipelago as his private fief with a presidential guard of thousands, converted to Islam, took the name Said Mustapha Mhadjou, was convicted in absentia in Paris for the 1989 assassination of President Ahmed Abdallah, convicted again in 2006 for the 1995 coup (five years, suspended), and died in Paris in 2007 at seventy-eight, having never served a day for the coups themselves [A: French court records; Wikipedia].

France's perennial tolerance of Denard — the "Françafrique" pattern of using deniable Frenchmen for deniable work — is the direct ancestor of every state-sponsor tolerance documented in this file.

## 2.3 Angola 1975: the war that taught the industry what not to be

When Portugal withdrew from Angola in 1975, Holden Roberto's FNLA hired roughly 200 foreign mercenaries — recruited in London, commanded by the ex-paratrooper **Costas Georgiou, "Colonel Callan"** — who performed so badly, and committed such atrocities, that their defeat became the founding cautionary tale. The **Luanda Trial of June–July 1976** ended with **thirteen mercenaries executed by firing squad**, including Georgiou and an American, Daniel Gearhart; others received sentences of five to thirty years [A: trial records]. The industry's founding generation — Executive Outcomes, formed thirteen years later in South Africa — built its entire sales pitch on the sentence: *we are not Callan's men*. Professionalism, not zeal, was the product.

## 2.4 Watchguard and the Cold War fixers

In 1965, **David Stirling — founder of the SAS — co-founded Watchguard International** in London with fellow ex-SAS officer John Woodhouse: training teams for friendly governments (Yemen royalists, Zambia, Sierra Leone), arms-and-personnel deals "mainly for the Gulf states," and a documented link to a failed 1971 plot to overthrow Muammar Gaddafi. Woodhouse resigned after disagreements; Stirling ceased active involvement in 1972. The seven-year arc — special-forces founders, training product, coup-adjacent work, founding-father exit — is the exact arc later replayed by Executive Outcomes, Blackwater, Wagner, and Spear [A/B: SAS-history literature].

In Washington, the same era produced the **CIA's long relationship with the Knights of Malta**: directors William Casey and John McCone were documented members of the Sovereign Military Order of Malta — a sovereign military order seated at the Vatican with uniformed knights and global humanitarian operations — and redacted Order-of-Malta testimony sits in the Iran-Contra hearing transcripts [S/A: AP 2014; trial literature]. This is relevant to the modern file for one reason: it is the *verified* layer beneath two decades of unverified claims about the order's role in private military affairs (treated in Part XVII).

## 2.5 The proto-PMC's ledger: what the ancestors established

By 1989 the ancestors had established every element of the modern trade: cross-border recruitment of veterans (Zouaves, Congo); the ideological wrapper (crusade, anti-communism, Françafrique); payment in concessions and resources (Katanga's mining); the coup as product (Comoros); the state's double game (France's tolerance of Denard; the CIA's order memberships); and the industry's self-cleaning myth (professionalism as the antidote to the Callan example). What changed in 1989 was the corporate form: the first modern firm arrived, and with it, limited liability.

# PART III — THE FIRST GENERATION: EXECUTIVE OUTCOMES AND THE CORPORATE MERCENARY

## 3.1 Executive Outcomes (1989–1998): the proof of concept

**Executive Outcomes** was founded in 1989 by **Eeben Barlow**, a former officer of the apartheid-era South African Defence Force and of the Civil Cooperation Bureau — the regime's covert unit. With **Michael Mullen**, Barlow recruited from the apartheid security apparatus that Nelson Mandela's government was dismantling — the 32 Battalion, Koevoet, the Reconnaissance Commandos — and built a force with a peak pool of two to three thousand men [A: War on the Rocks; GlobalSecurity].

The corporate anatomy mattered as much as the soldiers. EO was the operating subsidiary of **Strategic Resource Corporation**, Barlow's holding company, with affiliates including **Branch Mining** (the concession arm) and **Executive Outcomes Aviation** — later Ibis Air — whose principals included, at various times, Simon Mann, Tony Buckingham, Derek Williams, and Crause Steyl, with Lafras Luitingh as chief operating officer and Nic van der Bergh as later CEO. The business model was novel and became the industry's template forever after: **payment in resources**. Corporate clients included Heritage Oil, De Beers, Chevron, and Rio Tinto; governments paid in mining concessions [A: Greydynamics corporate history; Sentry reporting].

**Angola (1993):** about three hundred EO men, flying Mi-24 Hind gunships and Mi-8 transports, with a T-72 tank and BMP-2 armored vehicles, retook the Soyo oil installation for Heritage Oil and then trained and fought alongside the Angolan army against UNITA. The battlefield defeat of UNITA produced the Lusaka ceasefire; when Angola, under American and UN pressure, terminated the contract, the war resumed [A: academic and press record].

**Sierra Leone (1995):** in March 1995 roughly three hundred EO men — two Mi-24s, Mi-8s, BMP-2s, one T-72 — stopped the Revolutionary United Front's advance on Freetown, retook the Kono diamond fields, and forced the negotiations that produced the Abidjan peace process. When EO withdrew under international pressure, the RUF returned and conducted **"Operation No Living Thing,"** the January 1999 sacking of Freetown. The premature-withdrawal lesson — that a rented army's exit can be deadlier than its absence — became the industry's standard sales argument and its clients' standard regret [A: War on the Rocks; multiple].

EO dissolved on 31 December 1998 as South Africa's Regulation of Foreign Military Assistance Act took effect — a statute that *licensed* foreign military assistance rather than banning it. Barlow later chaired **STTEP International** (formed 2009 from EO alumni) and, on 27 November 2020 — after resigning the STTEP chairmanship — **relaunched the Executive Outcomes brand itself**, announcing "strategic partnerships with two companies" (unnamed) and a mission that included, in his words, exposing "media and intelligence whores." The EO website remained live as of 2025; its post-2020 operations are unverified [B: Greydynamics 2025; EO site].

## 3.2 Sandline and the scandals that made the industry famous

**Sandline International** (UK, 1996–2004), founded by Scots Guards veteran **Tim Spicer**, subcontracted EO for its best-known operation: the 1997 intervention in Papua New Guinea's Bougainville conflict, which triggered a near-coup, a parliamentary revolt, and the resignation of Prime Minister Sir Julius Chan — the "Sandline Affair." In 1998 came the "Arms to Africa" affair: Sandline shipped Bulgarian weapons to Sierra Leone's restored elected government in violation of a UK embargo — the Foreign Office knew; Spicer testified before Parliament; no one was prosecuted [A: parliamentary record].

Sandline's personnel later surfaced in the file's most famous coup attempt (3.3), and Spicer's own corporate path ran onward: Crisis and Risk Management (2000), Strategic Consulting International (2001), and then **Aegis Defence Services (2002)** — the firm that won a $293 million US Army contract in Iraq, endured the 2005 "trophy video" scandal, and was acquired by GardaWorld in 2015, whose government-services arm guards US embassies today. The lineage — Sandline → Aegis → GardaWorld Government Services — is the single cleanest line from the 1990s mercenary era to the sanitized 2020s, and every stage of it is documented [A: corporate records; Parts IV, XII].

## 3.3 The Wonga Coup (2004): the industry's greatest-hits disaster

In March 2004, **Simon Mann** — Sandline co-founder, former SAS officer, veteran of Oman and of EO/Ibis Air — attempted to overthrow President Teodoro Obiang of Equatorial Guinea, Africa's emerging oil power, and install the exiled opposition figure Severo Moto. The goal, per the trial record and Mann's own memoir: control of the oil money — "wonga."

**The mechanics, documented to named-individual level:** Mann's chartered Boeing 727 landed at Harare, Zimbabwe, on 7 March 2004 to collect about one hundred assault rifles and RPGs from Zimbabwe Defence Industries; Zimbabwean intelligence — working with South African authorities and a leak from inside the plot — arrested Mann and sixty-nine men on the tarmac. The advance team in Malabo, led by South African **Nick du Toit**, was rounded up shortly after [A: Wikipedia/Wonga coup records].

**The consequences:** Mann received roughly seven years in Zimbabwe for the weapons landing, was extradited to Equatorial Guinea in 2008, and was sentenced to thirty-four years for the coup itself; he was pardoned by Obiang on 3 November 2009 on "humanitarian grounds." Du Toit was sentenced to thirty-four years in Equatorial Guinea (pardoned later). The financier web — the "Wonga List" of about ninety-five names Equatorial Guinea pursued — included **Mark Thatcher**, who pleaded guilty in South Africa to funding the helicopter element (fined R3 million with a suspended sentence); the Syrian-Lebanese oil fixer **Ely Calil** (always denied, sued, died 2018); and alleged contributions attributed to figures including Jeffrey Archer (denied) [A: trial records; The Times obituary, May 2025].

Mann died in London on 8 May 2025, aged seventy-two, of a heart attack — closing the last living primary source on the EO–Sandline–Wonga lineage. His arc — decorated special-forces officer to convicted coup-plotter to pardoned security consultant — remains the cleanest single-person illustration of the sanctioned-mercenary-to-consultant pipeline [A: The Times].

## 3.4 The lineage moves on: STTEP, Pilgrims Africa, Saracen/Sterling

Barlow's **STTEP International** (2009–2020, chairman Barlow) took the EO doctrine to Nigeria in early 2015 — engaged by the Jonathan administration ahead of the February elections, roughly one hundred personnel rebuilt the army's strike force and, in a three-month operation, broke Boko Haram's hold on the northeast; a company figure called it ending the group's "six-year reign of terror in three months." Barlow warned the incoming Buhari government; the warnings were ignored; in November 2015 the army's 157 Battalion was overrun and forty-four soldiers killed — the file's clearest documented case of a client ignoring a contractor's lessons and losing what the contractor had won [A/B: SOFREP series; Wits academic paper].

A second South African firm, **Pilgrims Africa Ltd** — Lagos-registered, run by EO Sierra Leone veteran Cobus Claassens — fought alongside the Nigerian effort in 2015; its operator Leon Lotz was killed on 9 March 2015, reportedly by friendly fire from the Nigerian military. South Africa's defense minister threatened the returning men with arrest; none were prosecuted. Pilgrims Africa remains the cleanest documented case of jurisdiction-shopping defeating South Africa's anti-mercenary statute: banned at home, legal in Lagos [B: SOFREP; IOL].

And in Somalia, the lineage acquired a government of its own. **Saracen International** — registered to ex-EO officer Lafras Luitingh — built the **Puntland Maritime Police Force** from 2010; rebranded **Sterling Corporate Services** (Dubai-registered) under UN pressure in 2012 — a trainer's own account concedes that "most of the guys just changed t-shirts and jumped into a new company." The UN monitoring group documented a private army answering only to Puntland's president; the contract was terminated in September 2012; **Bancroft Global Development** — the Washington nonprofit founded by banking heir Michael Stock, which trained African Union troops in Somalia — assessed the leftover force and declined the training contract. The PMPF survived its builders, absorbed UAE funding, and was running anti-piracy and counter-ISIS operations with some 4,800 personnel as of 2024 — and, as documented in Part X, its Bosaso compound became a staging hub for Colombian mercenaries bound for Sudan's war in 2024–26. The facility outlived three corporate generations and became infrastructure for a fourth [S: UN SEMG; A: Small Wars Journal; B: Feral Jundi].

# PART IV — THE AMERICAN WAR: THE CONTRACTOR REPUBLIC, 1995–2021

The American wars created the largest privatized military labor market in history. The numbers set the scale: in Iraq, contractor numbers grew from roughly 163,000 (2004–08 period) to about 180,000 in July 2007 — **more than the US troop contingent** (about 21,000 Americans, 43,000 third-country nationals, 118,000 Iraqis). In Afghanistan in 2009 there were about 255,000 contractors. The State Department's protective detail for Ambassador Bremer was a $21 million contract in 2003; the Worldwide Personal Protective Services contract of 2004 ran to $488 million; at the peak, more than half of all US defense personnel spending went to contractors, and private military contracting invoices exceeded $50 billion a year — roughly a third of the defense personnel budget [S: Cato/CRS compilations; LA Times 2007].

## 4.1 MPRI and the licensed advisory (Croatia, 1995)

**Military Professional Resources Inc.** — founded 1987 by retired US Army generals — was the cleanest early case of state-licensed deniability. Under Pentagon license (ostensibly NATO Partnership for Peace preparation while a UN arms embargo was in force), a fourteen-man training team led by retired Major General John Sewall worked in Croatia from January to April 1995. Weeks after the team's departure, Croatia's army executed **Operation Storm** — the offensive that crushed the Serbian Krajina and sent some 200,000 Serbs fleeing. MPRI denied planning the operation; US, Croatian, and company officials maintained the classroom-only line; French and British officials accused MPRI of planning it; the New York Times reported allegations of doctrine advice, scenario planning, and satellite-intelligence sharing. What is documented is the pattern: a Pentagon-licensed American firm, a client army's decisive offensive, and no legal consequences for anyone [A: NYT; GlobalSecurity; S: CRS]. MPRI was sold to L-3 Communications for $40 million in 2000, folded into Engility, and absorbed into SAIC — the brand died; the business continued (see the pass-8 rebrand ledger, Part XIV).

## 4.2 DynCorp and the Bolkovac case

**DynCorp International** — an aviation-logistics company founded in 1946 — held logistics and police-training contracts across the Balkans under the UN's International Police Task Force. In 1999, American police officer **Kathryn Bolkovac**, hired through DynCorp's UK subsidiary, was assigned to UN Gender Affairs in Zenica, Bosnia, where she uncovered an extensive trafficking operation: women and girls from Russia, Romania, Moldova, and Ukraine — some as young as twelve — held in brothels patronized by international personnel, including DynCorp supervisors. A case study later summarized that roughly 30–40 percent of the brothels' clientele were international personnel. Bolkovac was demoted and fired in October 2000 after internal whistleblower Ben Johnston (fired 2000, settled August 2002) came forward; DynCorp fired seven employees for purchasing women, including underage girls. In a British employment tribunal she won unanimously in August 2002 and was awarded £110,221 in November 2002. **No criminal prosecutions followed** — an immunity ambiguity under the Dayton Accords left jurisdiction unclear, and the men were repatriated instead. The UN's Conduct and Discipline Unit was created only in 2007. DynCorp kept its contracts [S: UK tribunal record; ICoCA case map; A: contemporaneous reporting].

DynCorp itself continued through acquisitions — Global Linguist Solutions, CSRA, General Dynamics' IT arm — and was folded into **Amentum** in 2020–21. Amentum today is a $13-billion-revenue super-prime: 51 percent owned by Jacobs Solutions' public shareholders, 37–41.5 percent by the private-equity funds American Securities and Lindsay Goldberg, with a $50 billion backlog and 53,000 employees — the DynCorp lineage, fully sanitized [S: SEC information statement].

## 4.3 Blackwater: founding, growth, and the road to Nisour Square

**Blackwater** was founded in 1996 by Erik Prince — ex-Navy SEAL, heir to an auto-parts fortune, raised Dutch Reformed and later a Catholic convert — on a North Carolina training campus that became an industry symbol. By the Iraq war it held State Department protective contracts worth, in time, hundreds of millions of dollars; its men guarded the highest-value targets in Baghdad.

The defining incidents came early. On 31 March 2004, four Blackwater contractors were ambushed in Fallujah; their bodies were burned and hung from a bridge — the event that triggered the First Battle of Fallujah. In April 2004 Blackwater contractors helped break a Mahdi Army siege of the Coalition Provisional Authority compound in Najaf. The company's subsidiary web — Presidential Airways, Total Intelligence Solutions (led by former CIA counterterrorism chief Cofer Black), Paravant, AirScan — extended into aviation, intelligence, and risk consulting; by 2006 Cofer Black was publicly pitching a "brigade-size force for low-intensity conflicts at a fraction of the cost of NATO operations," a statement he later denied [A: corporate records; NYT; Cato].

Then came **Nisour Square, 16 September 2007**: a Blackwater convoy team designated Raven 23 opened fire in a Baghdad traffic circle and killed **seventeen Iraqi civilians**; twenty more were wounded. The FBI confirmed fourteen of the deaths were unjustified. In December 2008 the Justice Department charged five guards — Paul Slough, Nicholas Slatten, Evan Liberty, Dustin Heard, and Donald Ball — with thirty-five counts; one, Jeremy Ridgeway, pleaded guilty and cooperated. In 2014 Slatten was convicted of murder; Slough, Liberty, and Heard of manslaughter with thirty-year firearm minimums. In August 2017 the DC Circuit overturned Slatten's conviction and ordered resentencing; the others received twelve-to-fifteen-year terms. **On 22 December 2020, President Trump pardoned all four.** The UN Working Group on the Use of Mercenaries called the pardons a violation of US obligations under international law and "an affront to justice"; the Iraqi foreign ministry protested; Human Rights Watch wrote that justice had been undone "by the stroke of a pen" [S: DOJ releases; S: OHCHR, 30 Dec 2020].

The corporate sequence followed: Blackwater became **Xe Services** (February 2009), then **Academi** (December 2011), then merged with Triple Canopy and others into **Constellis** (2014), which Apollo Global Management acquired in 2016 (its international affiliate Greystone — a Barbados entity formed in 2010 and management-bought-out the same year — survives as the lineage's quietest subsidiary). Every capability — training, aviation, armored vehicles, protective services — continued under the new names. A 2026 examination of SEC filings (Part XII) shows the Apollo-era equity was largely destroyed in a 2020 restructuring; the firm now runs as a leveraged entity financed through business-development companies, with noteholders including the Kelso and Estancia funds — and, since March 2025, a former deputy commander of US Joint Special Operations Command, Vice Admiral Colin Kilrain, on its board [S: SEC BDC filings; A: PRNewswire].

## 4.4 Abu Ghraib and the CACI arc: the $42 million that lasted 168 days

At Abu Ghraib in 2003–04, **CACI** interrogators and Titan interpreters were implicated in the abuse documented by the Taguba and Fay reports. The civil case — **Al Shimari v. CACI** — was filed in 2008 under the Alien Tort Statute, survived more than twenty dismissal motions, went to trial in April 2024 (the twentieth anniversary of the scandal), and produced a hung jury; at retrial in November 2024 a federal jury in Virginia found CACI liable and awarded **$42 million** ($3 million compensatory plus $11 million punitive for each of three plaintiffs) — the first time a civilian contractor had been held liable for Abu Ghraib. The Fourth Circuit affirmed in full on 12 March 2026. **On 28 August 2026, after the Supreme Court's decision in Cisco v. Doe, the Fourth Circuit vacated the verdict** — holding that ATS conspiracy claims must be dismissed. As of this writing, the US accountability scorecard for the Iraq generation reads: four men pardoned, one verdict vacated, one settlement era ended, and zero final judgments against contractors [S: court records; A: CCR statements].

## 4.5 The going-native pattern at full depth: ArmorGroup and Mr. Pink

The Senate Armed Services Committee's 2010 report on Afghanistan records the canonical case of what happens when a contractor's local labor force becomes the real power. At Shindand Airbase, **ArmorGroup** — the G4S-lineage firm — sourced roughly half its guard force each from two feuding warlords known to company staff only as **"Mr. White" (Timor Shah) and "Mr. Pink" (Nadir Khan)** — nicknamed after Reservoir Dogs. They had been recommended by US special-operations personnel. On 12 December 2007, Pink's men murdered White at the bazaar; a company security manager's verdict on the record: "kind of like a mafia thing. If you rub somebody out, you'll get a bigger piece of the pie." **ArmorGroup met White's brother to recontract the following day.** Pink's militia subsequently fed coalition forces false intelligence pointing at the brother's compound; the resulting airstrike at Azizabad killed some ninety civilians, and a company email marveled that Pink was "pretty shrewd." Fifteen of Pink's men fired for passing airfield security information were **rehired within weeks by a rival contractor, EOD Technology**, under a separate US contract — the fired-by-one, hired-by-the-next flow that the 2024–26 Sudan pipeline would replicate exactly [S: SASC report, Sep 2010].

The same firm's Herat operation added the warlord-sourcing corollary: ArmorGroup hired the Taliban-linked strongman Matiullah Khan to supply its guards; the guards defected to the Taliban; the Senate's follow-up report ("Warlord Inc.," 2010) documented the protection-racket structure — the client paying its enemy's ally for the privilege of being guarded [S: SASC 2010].

## 4.6 The mid-tier and the domestic guard architecture

The Iraq-Afghanistan era's middle layer deserves its own page, because this is where the labor market learned its modern shape:

- **MVM Inc.** (founded 1979 by three ex-Secret Service agents): the CIA's personal-security prime — its program Viper (renamed Panther) and the NSA's "Scorpion" security program in Iraq; a 2004 incident in which its team, after being car-bombed, fired on Iraqi civilians was internally reported in a version contradicted by physical evidence, leading to a whistleblower suit; the State Department terminated its Kabul embassy-guard contract in 2005. Classified contracts were about 30 percent of revenue — roughly $60 million a year — as of 2007 [A: WSJ 2008].
- **Sallyport Global** (Reston, Virginia): more than $1 billion in US government contracts from 2014; a 2018 investigation documented the Balad Air Base's internal order — a South African guard cabal switching to Afrikaans to evade oversight (a direct echo of the apartheid-era lineage of Part III), an alleged "Bosnian mafia" controlling the motor pool, passports confiscated from third-country workers, and Iranian-backed militia presence inside the wire.
- **SOC LLC** (Reston, 2007): mobilized more than ten thousand security professionals in Iraq in 2004; today holds a position on the US Army's $10.3 billion guard-and-protective-services contract through 2035, an $83.6 million State Department award, and — as documented in Part IX — the security contract for the US diplomatic mission in Jerusalem since late 2016, with the embassy currently soliciting Israeli-certified tactical training for its guard force [S/A: USASpending; embassy solicitations].
- **TigerSwan**: the North Carolina firm founded by a former Delta Force officer, which infiltrated and compiled dossiers on Dakota Access Pipeline protesters in 2016–17 and was sued by North Carolina for operating without a license — the pipeline-protection niche of the US market.
- **Custer Battles**: the Iraq-contracting fraud convictions — the first of the war — followed by dissolution; the brand died, the principals moved on. Global Strategies Group — the UK-Dubai firm that replaced Custer Battles at Baghdad Airport and helped the Coalition Provisional Authority draft the industry's regulations — ran the same airport-through-2013 before its own corporate fate faded from the record [B: Powerbase].
- **Chenega Corporation** and the Alaska Native Corporations: the statutory set-aside architecture (8(a) of the Small Business Act) built a domestic armed-guard duopoly — Chenega grew from $13 million in revenue in 2000 to $1.1 billion by 2009 on no-bid contracts (Iraq reconstruction, Guantánamo security, airport X-ray maintenance), paying roughly $30,000 a year in dividends to its 170 Alaska Native shareholders while spending $2.7 million lobbying to protect the preference structure [S: GAO; NA-primary: ProPublica 2011].
- **The fatal-ledger**: by the end of 2012, roughly 3,000 contractors had died in Iraq, Afghanistan, and Kuwait — with employer-level attribution led by L-3/Titan/MPRI (373), Supreme Group (241), Compass (163), SEI (127), DynCorp (101), and Aegis (89). Contractor deaths ran to roughly 30 percent of total US battlefield losses — a workforce whose sacrifice has no memorial and, largely, no legal recourse [S/A: Cato, DoD records].

## 4.7 The Iraq-era lesson

The American war established three durable facts. First, the contractor could outnumber the soldier, and the policy apparatus — Bremer's **Coalition Provisional Authority Order 17**, granting contractors immunity from Iraqi law — could make him untouchable in the country where he worked. Second, accountability would run through American courts or nowhere: MEJA, the Military Extritorial Jurisdiction Act of 2000, was successfully used exactly once in two decades (for a child-pornography case, not violence); the War Crimes Act produced no contractor prosecutions; and the courts that did act saw their work undone by pardons and doctrine. Third, the industry's response to scandal — rename, restructure, continue — was validated at the largest possible scale: the firm whose men killed seventeen civilians in Nisour Square is, under its third name, guarding American embassies today.

# PART V — THE GULF MERCENARY MARKET

The United Arab Emirates — a federation of some 2.7 million citizens sitting on enormous oil wealth, surrounded by instability, and deeply casualty-averse — built the densest mercenary market on earth. The story of that market is the story of the industry's second generation: not armies for hire, but deniable specialists brokered through political fixers, paid in cash and concessions, and dissolved within days of exposure.

## 5.1 The broker: Mohammad Dahlan

At the center of the UAE market stands **Mohammad Dahlan** — a man whose biography spans every layer of this file. Born in Gaza's Khan Younis refugee camp, he rose to head Yasser Arafat's Preventive Security Force in Gaza in the 1990s, running what critics called "Dahlanistan": a twenty-thousand-man force, close CIA and Israeli liaison ties, and — per a documented 1997 scandal at the Karni crossing — the diversion of roughly 40 percent of crossing taxes (about a million shekels a month) to accounts he controlled. Expelled by Hamas in 2007, he surfaced in the UAE as a security adviser to Crown Prince Mohammed bin Zayed. As of January 2026 he remains an adviser to MBZ with a security-focused portfolio, and by May 2026 he was in direct liaison with Israel's Shin Bet chief in Abu Dhabi. The Wall Street Journal reported US, Israeli, and Arab officials viewing him as a leading candidate for postwar Gaza governance; in August 2026 the Telegraph reported that he "played a crucial role" in securing the Gaza disarmament roadmap, having helped draft it in coordination with Jared Kushner, and that he helped convince Hamas that only through disarmament could its political future be secured. He denies interest in elected office. His Montenegro corporate web — Levant International Inc DOO Podgorica, Manarah Holding, Queens Rich Development, Middle East Real Estate DOO — was financed, per a Palestinian judicial indictment, with stolen Palestinian unemployment funds; his longtime partner Mohammed Rachid, Arafat's economic adviser, was sentenced in 2012 to fifteen years for embezzling $33.5 million of Palestinian investment funds [A/B: indictment records]. [S/A: ECFR mapping; Telegraph; Al-Monitor; WSJ; Palestinian Authority indictment records; wealth estimates >$120M].

His thirty-year arc — militia commander to MBZ's security fixer to peace-plan negotiator — intersects every Gulf operation in this part: the Prince battalion, the Spear kill program, the Sudanese conscription pipeline, the Colombian labor supply, and the 2026 Gaza settlement. One man, every layer.

## 5.2 Reflex Responses (2010–2011): the battalion that vanished

In 2010 Erik Prince signed a $529 million contract to build an eight-hundred-man battalion — mostly Colombian special-forces veterans — for the UAE at Zayed Military City, trained by American, British, German, and French legion veterans. The New York Times exposed the program in May 2011: Prince operated under the codename "Kingfish," appeared on no documents, and — per the report — would not hire Muslims, on the theory that they could not be counted on to kill fellow Muslims. His US personnel were employed through a UAE entity called **Assurance Management Consultants**, which instructed them to use generic email signatures — an explicit end-run around American export-control law (ITAR) [S: NYT, 14 May 2011].

When the story broke, the UAE shut the program within days. Some $100 million was rolled into a successor entity, Vulcan Management (under an ex-US Army officer named Reno Alberto); Prince claimed he was owed upwards of $40 million, paid via a Bermuda-structured $5.9 million "loan." Emirati anti-piracy work continued separately with ex-EO officer Lafras Luitingh, whose Somalia program later showed about $50 million in unaudited gaps — Prince's own comment on the missing funds, preserved in the record: "I'm sure they are just saving it for a rainy day" [A: The Intercept; audit records].

The battalion capability did not die; it was absorbed. The UAE's ruling family ultimately built the institutional version of what Prince had prototyped — today visible in the training arms of the EDGE Group conglomerate (below, 5.7) and, as of 2024, in the Manar Military Company's planned foreign legion (5.6).

## 5.3 Spear Operations Group (2015–2018): the admitted assassination program

**Spear Operations Group LLC** was a Delaware-registered company founded by **Abraham Golan** — a Hungarian-Israeli dual citizen who marketed himself as a former French Foreign Legion and Israeli special-forces officer (claims BuzzFeed could not verify; his own recruitment materials dating to 2012 carried them). The firm's co-broker was **Isaac Gilmore**, a former US Navy SEAL, and its client chain ran through Mohammad Dahlan to the UAE's Presidential Guard.

The deal was struck over lunch at an Italian restaurant at a UAE military officers' club, brokered by Dahlan: **$1.5 million a month plus kill bonuses** — with the first operation half-price. The twelve-man team, assembled at a New Jersey hotel with three cases of whiskey, included an ex-CIA Ground Branch officer, a Maryland Army National Guard special-forces sergeant, a former Delta Force operator (**Dale Comstock**), and at least one SEAL Team Six reservist — Americans paid roughly double the French Foreign Legionnaires on the same team. The UAE issued them military ranks and low-grade Chinese weapons, an explicit deniability design [A: BuzzFeed News, 16 Oct 2018 — the foundational exposé; 2025-26 court filings].

From Aden, starting 29 December 2015, the team ran a campaign against figures in Yemen's Al-Islah movement. The first operation — a bomb attack on the office of **Anssaf Ali Mayo**, an Al-Islah leader and member of parliament — was meant, in Golan's words, to "kill everybody in that office." It failed. The team then shifted to magnetized-bomb motorcycle assassinations of Mayo's associates, killing local political figures including a school principal. Golan's on-the-record confession to BuzzFeed: **"There was a targeted assassination program in Yemen. I was running it. We did it. It was sanctioned by the UAE within the coalition."** In a 2024 BBC documentary, Gilmore and Comstock confirmed that the UAE provided kill-target cards, including Mayo's [A: BuzzFeed; BBC 2024].

**The litigation (as of September 2026):** Mayo sued Golan, Gilmore, and Comstock in federal court in San Diego — **Mayo v. Golan et al., 3:25-cv-03651, Judge Linda Lopez** — under the Alien Tort Statute for war crimes and attempted extrajudicial killing, filed 18 December 2025, unsealed March 2026, with the Center for Justice and Accountability as counsel. Golan and Gilmore, though served, never appeared; **default proceedings against them are live**, while Comstock litigates pro se. A separate case — **Alsanabani v. Spear Operations Group et al., D.D.C., 1:25-cv-01684** — brought Anti-Terrorism Act claims against the same network **plus Erik Prince, the Reflex Responses entities, and two banks (Bank of New York Mellon and First Abu Dhabi Bank's US arm)** on behalf of the family of a Yemeni-American student murdered at an Emirati-backed checkpoint in 2021. Judge John Bates dismissed Prince in December 2025 and **First Abu Dhabi Bank in April 2026, on the pleadings**; default motions against the remaining shells were filed on 11 September 2026. The two men who confessed on camera are headed toward default judgments; the banks that moved the money won at the pleadings; and the US Department of Justice has brought no criminal case despite the published admissions [S: dockets].

The complaint's texture, from the court papers and reporting: Comstock was hired with $40,000 cash on the table and paid $40,000 a month plus bonuses; Gilmore had been decommissioned from the Navy in 2011 after accidentally shooting a SEAL in a training exercise; Golan ran the program from a seven-million-dollar San Diego mansion and now lives in Westport, Connecticut; Comstock litigates from Indonesia. The complaint quotes the UN Panel of Experts' finding of "reasonable grounds to believe that the United Arab Emirates are responsible for the 10 assassinations in Aden it investigated" [A: Yahoo/California Post; NPR; dockets].

## 5.4 Black Shield and the conscription pipeline

**Black Shield Security** (UAE, est. 2001) ran a documented conscription fraud: Sudanese migrants were recruited in Khartoum through employment agencies with "security guard" contracts and UAE embassy-sealed papers, flown to the Emirates, militarily trained, and then forced into deployments to Libya and Yemen — some as child soldiers. Human Rights Watch documented the pattern in November 2020 ("Recruited as Security Guards in the UAE, Deceived into Working in Conflict-Ridden Libya Instead"); families protested outside the UAE embassy in Khartoum ("Our sons are not for sale"); Sudanese courts opened proceedings against complicit travel agencies. The UAE's Hemedti connection ran deeper: thousands of Sudanese from the Rapid Support Forces — the paramilitary commanded by **Mohamed Hamdan "Hemedti" Dagalo**, whose family dominates Sudan's gold sector — were deployed to Libya from 2019 with Emirati support, earning Hemedti's network hundreds of millions of dollars [S: HRW; A: MEE, Anadolu].

## 5.5 The GSSG pipeline (2024–2026): the industry's best-documented live operation

**Global Security Services Group** — registered in Abu Dhabi in 2016, owner and chief executive **Mohamed Hamdan al-Zaabi**, holding what it calls the UAE's first armed private-security license — sits at the employer end of the pipeline this document can trace furthest. The recruiter is **A4SI** (International Services Agency), a Colombian company founded in 2017 by retired Major Omar Antonio Rodríguez and controlled since 2022 by **Claudia Oliveros**, wife of the pipeline's field commander.

That commander is **Álvaro Quijano** — a retired Colombian battalion commander in the UAE's own Seventh Special Forces Brigade (by 2014), a dual Colombian-Italian national, and, per the US Treasury's designation statement, a **former associate of the Norte del Valle cartel**. US sanctions of 9 December 2025 (under Executive Order 14098) named Quijano, A4SI, Oliveros, and the Panama-registered payroll conduit **Global Staffing** — the company through which Abu Dhabi salaries were routed to Colombian accounts, Oliveros a director. The United Nations followed on 28 April 2026, sanctioning Quijano and his wife [S: Treasury; UN].

The route, established by phone-tracking of more than fifty devices and confirmed by two former contractors to Agence France-Presse: **Bogotá → Abu Dhabi** (training at the Ghiyathi base, a UAE state military facility; recruits told they would guard oil infrastructure) → **the PMPF compound at Bosaso, Puntland** — the very facility Part III left behind in 2012 — as a staging hub → N'Djamena's military airfield in Chad → **South Darfur**, to a base fifty kilometers north of Nyala whose WiFi networks were named "DRONES" and "LOBOS DEL DISIERTO" — the Desert Wolves, Quijano's drone-centric brigade embedded with the Rapid Support Forces. The Financial Times puts the total at roughly **two thousand Colombians**. They were paid $2,500–4,000 a month — up to six times a Colombian army pension — through the Panama conduit, under contracts that bore "security guard" titles and confidentiality clauses; some, per the UN Fact-Finding Mission, were not informed of their destination and were paid less than contracted [S: HRW, May 2026; CIG; A: FT, France24].

The impact ledger: the Desert Wolves were geolocated in **El Fasher** during the October 2025 takeover — the siege's death toll estimated at up to 60,000, with satellite-based estimates running to 150,000, the largest massacre of the twenty-first century by those estimates. Contractors participated, per their own later testimony; some trained child recruits. In July 2026, Associated Press reported Colombians retreating to bunkers during airstrikes, leaving RSF prisoners shackled to vehicles [S: Yale HRL; ACLED; A: AP; S: UN FFM A/HRC/63/23].

The accountability cascade: US sanctions December 2025; UK sanctions February 2026 (three Colombians plus commanders); a **European Parliament resolution passed 9 July 2026, 476–28**, naming the UAE and calling for GSSG sanctions; the EU Council followed on 13 July with its first-ever Sudan gold-sector measures — a ban on Sudanese gold imports and on mercury and sodium-cyanide exports — aimed squarely at the war's financing. Colombia's foreign minister formally apologized; Congress passed an anti-mercenarism law in March 2026. The European Center for Constitutional and Human Rights filed criminal complaints. And the pipeline's response followed the industry's oldest reflex: within days of the US designations, **Oliveros and an associate registered Zeuz Global in the UK** — a hiring entity already pre-registered (as "ODP8") in April 2025, eight months before the sanctions, the escape hatch built in advance [B: Guardian relay; S: sanctions].

Two corporate details complete the anatomy: GSSG's website listed the interior, foreign, and Presidential Affairs ministries as clients — the Presidential Affairs portfolio is the ruler's own court — until the entries were removed after AFP's queries; and twenty-six authorizations signed by the contractors in eastern Libya empowered GSSG to pay salaries through the Panama conduit. Quijano, meanwhile, lobbied Chile's state copper commission on GSSG's behalf in 2024 — the pipeline's commander marketing it to mining clients in Latin America [A: AFP; Sentry].

## 5.6 Project Opus (2019): eighty million dollars and a hit list

In April 2019 Erik Prince pitched the Libyan warlord Khalifa Haftar — in Cairo, four days before President Trump publicly endorsed Haftar — an eighty-million-dollar operation: armed helicopters, drones, maritime interdiction, medical evacuation, intelligence, and a JSOC-modeled **"termination team,"** with a planning document listing ten assassination targets including two European citizens. The helicopters — nine surplus American-made aircraft (six MD530 Little Birds, three AH-1 Cobras) — were to come from Jordan, organized by Prince's associate **Prince Faisal ibn al-Hussein** of the Jordanian royal family; fraudulent end-user certificates (destination falsified as Tunisia) were prepared. The CIA learned of claims of White House approval and told King Abdullah to stop it; the king did. In June 2019 a twenty-man team nonetheless flew to Benghazi with six unarmed replacement helicopters bought from South Africa; Haftar, enraged, threatened them; the mercenaries escaped by rigid-hulled inflatable boat on a thirty-six-hour run to Malta, where twenty were briefly detained and released. The cover story was an $85 million counterfeit geological-survey contract with **Bridgeporth** — a company in which Prince owned a share — the same cover used for his earlier Project BROOKLYN kill-or-capture plan against Joseph Kony. Shell companies **Lancaster 6 DMCC, L-6 FZE, and Opus Capital Asset Limited FZE** (UAE) carried the operation; a Maltese arms dealer leased the getaway boats; a British businesswoman in Dubai was named for sanctions violations [S: UN Panel of Experts report, February 2021; A: NYT, WSJ, The Intercept].

Who paid the $80 million? The UN found Haftar's side paid it — the Libyan National Army, itself backed at the time by the UAE and Russia. Prince was never charged; at least four countries opened criminal probes per Western officials; the FBI examined the weaponized crop-duster exports (the LASA T-Bird — a Thrush 510 secretly fitted with rocket and cannon pods; only two exist, both his). Prince's lawyer denied everything; his associate's lawyer called it "oil and gas logistics." As of this writing, the only confirmed consequence is documentary [S/A: as cited].

## 5.7 The state platform: IGG, EDGE, and the Manar legion

**International Golden Group** (est. 2002; a thousand-plus employees of dozens of nationalities; services including "military and security training") joined the UAE's **EDGE Group** in January 2024. EDGE — the 2019 consolidation of twenty-five defense entities — reports about $5 billion in revenue (2025), 70 percent of it exported (from $50 million of exports in 2019), a $20.4 billion backlog, nineteen thousand employees, and twenty-five joint ventures including Anduril; its investors are state holding companies (Mamoura/ADQ, Tawazun). CEO Hamad Al Marar told Monocle in June 2026: "I can tell you clearly: we have never supplied Sudan" — against the documented UN and Amnesty findings of Emirati weapons in the war, an entry for the denial ledger [S/A: Aviation Week; Monocle].

And in 2024, the market's institutional endpoint surfaced: **Manar Military Company**, an Abu Dhabi firm owned fifty-fifty by brothers Saeed and Nawwaf Ghobash, run by CEO **Freddy Riaud**, a former French naval commando and ex-adviser to the UAE Presidential Guard. Intelligence Online's March 2024 reporting — based on a leaked recruitment document — described a plan to recruit three to four thousand foreign ex-soldiers as a standing formation inside the UAE armed forces: a French Foreign Legion model, with $2,000-a-month starting pay, intended for Yemen, Somalia, and Sudan, under the Presidential Guard's umbrella. After the leak, Riaud conceded the project had "existed" but claimed it stopped; analysts assessed it went deeper underground, and as of this writing Manar is openly hiring a sales-and-business-development manager in Abu Dhabi. The Ghobash family's parallel moves — including a February 2025 investment in Mali gold — place the family in the same resource-security symbiosis this document tracks from Executive Outcomes forward [A: Intelligence Online; DW; B: Al-Estiklal].

## 5.8 The 2026 war and the Gulf demand catalog

The February 2026 war with Iran converted the Gulf mercenary market from discretionary to structural. The documented demand: with some 2,000 Iranian missiles and 4,400 drones striking Gulf targets in six weeks — the UAE absorbing roughly 90 percent — Gulf states began recruiting, through intermediaries, **radar operators, electronic-warfare specialists, ground-maintenance crews, ground security teams, and limited pilots, with a preference for Muslim-majority-country personnel and European engineers** [A: Middle East Eye, 9 Mar 2026]. The industrial surge answered the same demand: a $3 billion deal with Lockheed Martin and Northrop Grumman signed 3 August 2026 to triple Patriot production and quadruple THAAD; the UAE's $211 million THAAD launcher contract; and the unprecedented operational integration — Israeli Iron Dome crews deployed inside the Emirates (Part IX), and the Vectus ADS "air defense as a service" venture (Part VIII) waiting with a $600–720 million supplier agreement and, as of this writing, no announced client [S/A: Reuters; Part IX sources].

The Gulf market's lesson, stated plainly: the state that spent fifteen years renting deniable battalions has arrived at the institutional legion — and the first great-power war to stress-test the model found it ordering contractors by the skill-set, at war prices, in the open.

# PART VI — THE RUSSIAN ECOSYSTEM: FROM SLAVONIC CORPS TO THE CORPS SYSTEM

## 6.1 Origins: Moran, Slavonic, and the men who became Wagner

The Russian private-military tradition begins in the grey zone between veterans' entrepreneurship and state direction. **Moran Security Group** (St. Petersburg, ex-GRU officers; maritime security) spawned **Slavonic Corps Ltd**, registered in Hong Kong in 2012–13, which recruited about 267 men through Syria's 2013 decree legalizing private military companies, promising $5,000 a month to guard oil fields. The mission collapsed after an Islamic State ambush near Deir ez-Zor; in 2014 a Russian court sentenced Slavonic's managers in what the Russian press called the country's first-ever mercenary convictions — a signal that the venture had been unsanctioned adventurism rather than state policy [A: RFE/RL, court records].

From those ashes, in May 2014, came **PMC Wagner**. The commander was **Dmitry Utkin** — born 1970 in Asbest, a GRU lieutenant colonel from the 700th Spetsnaz Detachment, a two-war Chechnya veteran with neo-Nazi tattoos (the "Wagner" callsign; the name stuck). The financier and director was **Yevgeny Prigozhin**, "Putin's chef," whose Concord catering empire held billions in Russian state school and military contracts. The two formed company-tactical groups in Rostov-on-Don in May 2014 — "Luna" and "Step" — assembled around GRU cores, and within weeks were fighting at Luhansk Airport, where seventy-two Wagner men engaged and fifteen died, and at Debaltseve in 2015, where two hundred five men fought and twenty-one died. Pay ran about 180,000 rubles a month; the real casualty rate, by one veteran's account, ran 15–20 percent. In June 2014, an off-road team with a 9K38 Igla missile shot down a Ukrainian Il-76 over Luhansk, killing forty-nine — mostly paratroopers. It remains the group's deadliest single action and one of its least remembered [A: Jamestown; Meduza; Novaya Gazeta].

The first-year record also includes the file's first alleged intra-militia assassination attributed to Wagner: the December 2014 car-bomb killing of the Cossack commander Pavel Dremov — assessed by investigators as "either the Russian military or (more likely) Wagner" — three years before the Syria "hammer" videos made the practice famous, and Ukrainian security-service audio suggesting a Wagner role in the MH17 airspace environment. Wagner's corporate footprint reached Madagascar (election-season operations from April 2018) and Argentina (a Future Forge registration with St. Petersburg and Hong Kong offices) — the Southern-Hemisphere shell network [A: SSU; IStories].

## 6.2 Syria: Palmyra, oil, and Khasham

Wagner's Syrian deployment from 2015 produced its three signature episodes. First, the recapture of **Palmyra** (March 2016 and again 2017) — the propaganda masterpiece that made the group famous. Second, the oil: through Prigozhin-linked entities such as **Evro Polis**, the group secured a January 2017 contract giving it 25 percent of production from "liberated" fields — backed by the Russian Energy Ministry — with Wagner's "ISIS Hunters" providing the security. Third, **the Battle of Khasham, 7 February 2018**: a five-hundred-man pro-Assad force heavy with Wagner men, tanks included, crossed the Euphrates deconfliction line toward the Conoco gas plant, where about forty American special-operations troops and their Kurdish partners held the position. US deconfliction calls to the Russians were denied; over four hours, American artillery, Apache helicopters, AC-130s, F-22s, MQ-9s, and B-52s destroyed the assault force — two to three hundred killed, per Pentagon documents, at the cost of zero American or Kurdish casualties. Defense Secretary Mattis's order, on the record: the force was to be "annihilated... and it was." It was the first direct US-Russia kinetic engagement of the modern era, and Prigozhin's grievance over it — plus an ammunition feud with the defense minister — became part of the mutiny's background music [A: War on the Rocks; Pentagon documents].

## 6.3 Africa: the model and its ledger

Wagner's African business became the template the whole industry studied. In **Sudan** from 2017, Prigozhin-linked **M-Invest** managed the al-Ibaidiya ("Meroe") gold complex — exclusive purchasing rights over artisanal output, bullion airlifted to Dubai through the Meroe Gold subsidiary — while Wagner trained the Rapid Support Forces in Darfur. Leaked documents show M-Invest paying millions to a Sudanese military-intelligence company and the firm's 30-percent profit share waived by decree in 2018. Investigators estimate that 32.7 metric tons of gold — some $1.9 billion, roughly equal to Sudan's entire legitimate 2022 export volume — was smuggled out in a single year, with one planeload's manifest declaring "cookies" [S: OCCRP; Treasury; CNN].

In the **Central African Republic** from 2018, Wagner protected President Touadera personally, trained the army, fought off rebels at Bangui in January 2021, and took mining concessions through **Lobaye Invest** and **Midas Resources** — the Ndassima gold zone alone valued upward of $1 billion by a UK parliamentary inquiry. In **Libya** from 2019, Wagner supported Khalifa Haftar's LNA — at some two thousand men in 2020, withdrawing many to Ukraine in 2022. In **Mozambique** in 2019, about two hundred men deployed to protect Total's gas project, lost men to ambush, clashed with the client over tactics, and withdrew within months. In **Mali** from 2021, Wagner became the junta's counterinsurgency partner — the contract reportedly worth some $200 million in cash, with the Malian government insisting on keeping Wagner even as Moscow pushed its successor structures.

The human ledger, documented by Human Rights Watch, the UN, Amnesty, and the Sentry: the **Moura massacre** of 27–31 March 2022 — Malian forces and about a hundred white fighters killed an estimated 300–500 people, the UN later finding "strong indications" of conduct displaying the hallmarks of genocide, with 238 victims identified by name; a torture-camp system across six Malian bases documented in a 2025 joint investigation (arbitrary detention, starvation, container confinement, near-fatal torture); in CAR, a Sentry investigation documented "widespread, systematic, well-planned campaigns of mass killing, torture and rape," a parallel army, and a field motto — "leave no trace" [S: UN; HRW; A: Sentry, Forbidden Stories].

## 6.4 Ukraine: Bakhmut and the convict army

The 2022 invasion transformed Wagner into a mass-consumption product. From July 2022, Prigozhin personally toured Russian prisons recruiting; the resulting convict army — documented by BBC Russian and Mediazona through dog-tag analysis — numbered **at least 48,366 prisoners recruited, of whom 17,251 (35 percent) were confirmed killed by June 2024**; Prigozhin himself conceded roughly 20,000 dead, half of them convicts. Wave tactics, barrier troops, and summary execution for unauthorized retreat defined the Bakhmut "meat grinder," which the group took at ruinous cost in May 2023. Survivors received pardons — and a documented wave of reoffending followed, including a mass stabbing in Karelia in August 2023. The Russian Ministry of Defence copied the model as Storm-Z and Storm-V; by 2024–25 the prison pool was largely exhausted [A: BBC Russian; ISW].

## 6.5 Mutiny, crash, and the two corps

The endgame: a June 2023 decree required all volunteer formations to sign defense-ministry contracts. Prigozhin refused. On 23–24 June 2023 Wagner seized Rostov-on-Don and marched on Moscow — the "March of Justice" — halting about two hundred kilometers out under a Belarus-brokered deal. On 23 August 2023, an Embraer jet carrying Prigozhin, Utkin, and eight others crashed outside Tver; the FSB blamed a grenade. The nationalization followed: fighters signed ministry contracts or went to Belarus, where a camp at Tsel hosted thousands (its corporate registrations — a PMC Wagner LLC and a Concord entity with a combined authorized capital of about eighty dollars — are on record at the camp's address).

What emerged by December 2023 was **Africa Corps** — formally the Expeditionary Corps' African arm — under Deputy Defense Minister **Yunus-bek Yevkurov** and GRU General **Andrei Averyanov** (the officer associated with GRU Unit 29155, the sabotage-and-poisoning directorate). Seventy to eighty percent ex-Wagner; open Telegram recruiting; the men are legal Russian servicemen. Ukraine-facing formations consolidated under a "Volunteer Corps" affiliated with the National Guard, whose commander, Anton Elizarov, was EU-sanctioned for the Soledar assault. Russian analysts catalogued **thirty-seven Russian PMCs** operating abroad, of which the documented siblings include **Redut** (rebuilt by 2023 to roughly seven thousand men from prisoners and Central Asian migrants, under defense-ministry contract), **PMC Patriot** (sanctioned by the UN and others), **Convoy** (a GRU logistics-fixer's formation; Konstantin Pikalov, "Mazay," a veteran of GRU radiobiology experiments and of 2014 election operations in Bosnia, has headed it per sanctions designations), **RSB Group** (mine clearance and training), **Moran Security** (still active), and the **E.N.O.T. Corps** (dissolved 2019 after running a paramilitary youth camp on Serbia's Zlatibor mountain — its founder, Igor Mangushev, later killed in Ukraine in 2023) [S/A: DIIS; Jamestown; EU regulations; CEP].

## 6.6 The purge and the liquidation variant

The state's answer to the rebrand problem, Russian-style, is execution of the brand-owner. The network's afterlives are themselves documented: a columbarium at Goryachy Klyuch whose stele bears twenty thousand Wagner dog-tag numbers (a Mediazona satellite study, March 2026); a recruitment pivot — reported by the Financial Times in February 2026 — from war service to recruiting economically vulnerable Europeans for sabotage operations on NATO territory ("using the talent it has got available"); and a leaked-US-intelligence episode from the pre-Vectus period: Wagner itself pitched Haiti's government an anti-gang operation before Prince's firm won the contract — two rival ecosystems bidding the same product to the same failed state [A: FT; Mediazona; Wikipedia-compiled record].

And the church founded one too: **PMC Andreevsky Krest** (St. Andrew's Cross), created in 2017 under the Russian Orthodox Church — tactical, military-special, and "spiritual" training, its own volunteer battalions, sanctioned by the EU in December 2023. ## 6.9 PMC Española: the full dossier of the liquidation variant

The "Spanish" mystery solved: **PMC Española** was never Spanish. It was a Russian football-hooligan formation founded in spring 2023 by **Stanislav Orlov** — "Ispanec" ("the Spaniard"), a nom de guerre from his language skills — a CSKA Moscow ultra who had fought in Donbas from 2014 under the GRU-linked separatist commander Igor Bezler. # PART VII — TURKEY, IRAN, AND THE MIDDLE EASTERN LAYERS

## 7.1 SADAT: the Islamist counter-model

**SADAT International Defense Consultancy** was founded in Istanbul on 28 February 2012 by retired Brigadier General **Adnan Tanriverdi** — dismissed from the Turkish armed forces in 1997 for Islamist leanings, later President Erdogan's chief military adviser from 2016 to 2020 — together with twenty-two other retired officers. His son, **Melih Tanriverdi**, chairs the company; its stated doctrine is a "joint Islamic army" to end Muslim dependence on what Tanriverdi called Western crusader dominance [A: National Interest; company records].

The documented operations: a first Libya contact in May 2013; the shipment of roughly ten thousand tons of weapons to Tripoli-aligned militias in 2019; and, per the US Africa Command and the UN Panel of Experts, the supervision and payment of some five thousand Syrian fighters transferred to Libya to defend the UN-recognized government — men from named rebel factions flown from Gaziantep through Istanbul to Tripoli and Misrata on three-to-six-month contracts with Turkish protection papers and passport incentives. The UN documented SADAT's embargo violations; a UN special-rapporteur letter accused it of facilitating the selection and documentation of fighters, including children. SADAT instructors also trained Syrian proxies in the Afrin corridor and the anti-PKK Roj-Peshmerga in Iraqi Kurdistan, and supervised the Azerbaijani side's Syrian-fighter contingent (1,500–2,000 men) in the 2020 Karabakh war — the named source units (Hamza Division, Sultan Murad, Suqur al-Sham, Mu'tasim, Faylaq al-Sham, Ahrar al-Sharqiyah, Suleyman Shah) flown Gaziantep→Istanbul→Tripoli and Misrata on Turkish papers [S: UN; AFRICOM; A: Nordic Monitor, Le Monde].

## 7.2 The Iranian architecture: legions, cells, and outsourced coercion

Iran runs the largest state-organized deniable-force economy in the file. The **Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps** — allocated more than $6 billion in Iran's 2025 budget, nearly twice the conventional armed forces — fields the **Quds Force** and, through it, the **Shia Liberation Army**: Afghan and Pakistani divisions recruited on economic motivation, led after Qassem Soleimani's killing by Esmail Qaani. ## 7.3 Yemen 2026: the collapse of the UAE model and the return of the war

The UAE's two-decade Yemen project — the battalions, the assassination program, the conscript pipeline, and the thirty-thousand-man Giants Brigades built as local national armies — collapsed within a single year. Saudi Arabia relaunched its maritime coalition and airstrikes; in late September Britain's new prime minister committed RAF Voyager refueling tankers flying from Cyprus to support the Saudi air campaign — the first British military deployment of his government [S/A: UK Commons Library, Sep 2026; Reuters; Time].

# PART VIII — THE UKRAINE-ERA NEW WAVE (2022–2026)

## 8.1 Mozart: the control case, and the lesson it teaches

**Mozart Group** was founded in Kyiv in March 2022 by retired US Marine Colonel **Andrew Milburn** (former deputy commander of US Special Operations Command Central; former commander of the Marine Raider Regiment) and US Marine Reserve Colonel **Andrew Bain** (a thirty-year Ukraine businessman). Its structure was instructive: Milburn held 49 percent, Bain 51 percent; a Wyoming LLC; an affiliated 501(c)(3), Task Force Sunflower, served as the donation funnel. It trained Ukrainian special-operations forces, evacuated civilians from encircled cities (its trademark service, performed free while commercial extraction ran $10,000 a head), and ran battlefield clearance. It took no government money — Milburn said he was "stiff-armed because everyone remembers Blackwater" — and burned roughly $170,000–175,000 a month on donations, raising more than a million dollars.

The Russian targeting was explicit: Prigozhin publicly claimed Milburn commanded a Ukrainian brigade at Bakhmut; the group's sites were hit by missiles and DDoS; Milburn was described as first on a Wagner kill list. The collapse was internal: in late 2022 Bain demanded a $5 million buyout for his 51 percent; when the money could not be raised, the partners went to war — a December 2022 break-in at the Kyiv headquarters, a January 2023 lawsuit in Wyoming alleging charity misrepresentation and ITAR violations (training a foreign military without a State Department license), and a bitter public exchange that ended with Milburn declaring "today was the last day for the Mozart Group" on 31 January 2023. ## 10.3 The Congo: the industry's world fair

The eastern Democratic Republic of Congo hosts the file's only theater where every ecosystem operates simultaneously: **Vectus Global** (Erik Prince's firm, under the publicly confirmed "$100 million US-backed mining guard" launched April 2026, with an Israeli-advised Uvira drone operation in early 2026 and a Bujumbura air bridge); **Agemira RDC** (French-Congolese-founded; Bulgarian, Belarusian, Georgian, and other specialists rebuilding airfields and providing intelligence support); **Congo Protection** (Romanian, led by a former French Foreign Legion officer, a thousand men at peak — some surrendered at Goma and were evacuated to Kigali); UN-documented Latin American and other paramilitaries whose employers Kinshasa withholds; government-rehired operators of Chinese CH-4 drones (their private contract terminated in July 2025, the men rehired as state employees); Indian-made counter-drone systems under foreign management; **Africa Corps** pursuing an advisory footprint; and, on the other side, the M23 (March 23 Movement) rebellion backed by some three to four thousand Rwandan troops (denied), over the Rubaya coltan zone that supplies an estimated 15–30 percent of world coltan. The UN's arms embargo violations are documented both ways; the African Court and the International Court of Justice both hold live files [S/A: Jamestown; UN; CRS].

The pattern the Congo proves: privatization does not replace the state; it layers on top of it. Kinshasa's roster of foreign fighters exists alongside the army, the Wazalendo militia umbrella, MONUSCO, and the Rwandan army — seven armed architectures in one province, with the coltan as the prize [A: Security Council Report].


## 10.4 Haiti: the permanent urban air campaign

Haiti is where the industry crossed its final threshold — a private company running a standing drone-based targeted-killing campaign against a domestic criminal insurgency. After the Kenya-led Multinational Security Support mission faltered — four hundred Kenyans in June 2024, two hundred more in July, a Jamaican contingent, then Guatemala, El Salvador, and a final 217 Kenyans in January 2025, the same day Washington froze its funding; three Kenyan officers were killed; no territory was secured and no gang leader arrested; Kenya formally ended its deployment in 2026, the Haitian government contracted **Vectus Global** — the Erik Prince-linked firm — under a one-year order-maintenance contract (about $44 million by reporting; a ten-year customs-and-immigration-reform framework alongside it, under which the company would take a percentage of increased customs revenue plus a guaranteed share of import volumes regardless of performance — the fiscal-mercantile clause). Roughly two hundred personnel from the United States, Europe, and El Salvador operate explosive-armed quadcopters, helicopters, boats, and sniper teams alongside a Haitian task force operating outside normal police oversight; the US State Department confirmed an export license and simultaneously stated the US government has no oversight of the operations [S/A: HRW; Reuters; Militarnyi].

The kill chain, on the record: Human Rights Watch documented **141 drone operations between March 2025 and January 2026, killing at least 1,243 people and injuring 738 — including at least seventeen children and forty-three adults with no gang affiliation**; the UN human-rights office found 61 percent of all casualties occurred during security-force operations and characterized the drone strikes as "targeted killings... inconsistent with international human rights law." The tempo doubled over the period; the deadliest single strike killed fifty-seven; in one September 2025 strike on a gift distribution, nine civilians died including three children. No major gang leader was killed or captured. CSIS's Melissa Ziemer called it — accurately, per the data — "one of the deadliest air campaigns we have ever seen in the Western Hemisphere, far surpassing U.S. boat strikes in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific", surpassing US boat strikes in the Caribbean. A US congressional hearing grilled a Pentagon colonel over who authorizes Vectus's strikes ("Is it classified information?"); the Pentagon moved to "formalize coordination" among the UN-authorized Gang Suppression Force, the Haitian task force, and Vectus; a UN-authorized replacement force (the 5,500-strong GSF, UNSC Resolution 2793) began arriving in spring 2026 [S: HRW, OHCHR A/HRC/61/74, BINUH; S: UNSC Res 2793].

The Haitian verdict, stated without embellishment: a foreign company whose contract includes a share of customs revenue is conducting lethal air operations in a city of 2.7 million, under a US export license, with no disclosed targeting criteria, no oversight claim from the licensing state, a UN characterization of the practice as likely-unlawful targeted killing, and a documented record of zero strategic effect against the gangs — while the gang coalition controlling most of Port-au-Prince has been formally designated a foreign terrorist organization. The Nisour Square precedent — guards panicking in a traffic circle — has been replaced at scale: a company killing by algorithm, monthly, with a revenue-sharing clause in the treasury.

## 10.5 The seas: escorts, tolls, and the chokepoint as subscription

The maritime layer of the industry passed through three ages. The piracy era (2008–2013) built the private-armed-guard model — at its peak more than two hundred privately contracted armed security personnel companies, standards (ISO 28007), floating armories, and the UK-centric firms (Protection Vessels International, Typhon, MAST, GoAGT) escorting convoys; the Red Sea crisis of 2023–26 briefly revived the trade, with the Ambrey firm logging five hundred to six hundred transits a month at the height, until Houthi control of the Bab el-Mandeb approaches after September 2026 made the escort's destination enemy coastline — the market dying of strategic success by its adversary [A: Ambrey records]. The 2025–26 ledger of the escorts themselves deserves naming: the Magic Seas (sunk July 2025; four crew dead, eleven missing; its embarked security team destroyed two drone boats before the loss), the Eternity C (sunk days later; three killed), and the Tihamah (August 2026 at Bab el-Mandeb; six dead including two rescuers) — guard teams now standard aboard merchantmen and still insufficient against missile and drone swarms.

The same waters host the file's "black market PMC" tier (the African Defense Forum's category): the documented **weapons-for-piracy exchange between the Houthis and al-Shabaab**, Iranian-supplied arms moving to Somali waters in return for maritime pressure, and the January 2025 ISSOM drone strikes — marketized violence without corporate wrappers, the criminal complement of the escort industry [A/B: ADF].

The Iran War then produced the industry's purest new product: **the chokepoint as a subscription**. Iran's parliament legislated transit fees of up to 7 percent of cargo value with a twenty-percent fine for violators; Oman answered with a voluntary-fee regime modeled on the Strait of Malacca; Washington insisted no country may charge for international waterways while blockading the same strait. Analysts noted what the lawyers would argue for years: a "voluntary" fee collected under the shadow of mines and interception warnings "can become mandatory in practice," and — in the record's most telling sentence — no unilateral move to demand fees simply to traverse a strait has been made in modern history. The war's truest innovation was not the toll but the administration: physical passage, like physical security, packaged, priced, and permisson-gated [S/A: NPR, Reuters, Forbes, BBC].

## 10.6 Latin America: the drone war comes home and the mercenary export reverses

Two Latin American threads complete the outposts. First, the reverse pipeline: Mexican cartels — the CJNG with a dedicated drone-operator unit wearing custom insignia — now send members to Ukraine for drone training, while the US Drug Enforcement Administration counts 670 weaponized-drone events in the region since 2018, more than 72 percent of them since January 2025; SOUTHCOM has stood up an Autonomous Warfare Command to answer. The war that taught the world's contractors has begun teaching the world's cartels [A/B: CSIS, WaPo, ACLED, Vice].

Second, the Venezuela file: Silvercorp USA's 2020 "Operation Gideon" — the Florida firm's amphibious raid by two American ex-Green Berets and Venezuelan defectors under a signed contract with representatives of Juan Guaidó — ended with the Americans sentenced to twenty years and freed in a 2023 prisoner exchange; the firm's chief remains under US federal arms-trafficking investigation. And the file's 2026 coda: the state captured Maduro itself (Part IX.5), and the exiled Venezuelan security market — gang-suppression strikes, oil-field security, reconstruction protection — reopened under US auspices, with the industry's think-tank briefs already describing the client list [S/A: court records; Martens Center].

Ecuador opened a second Western-hemisphere chapter: in March 2025 President Noboa announced a "strategic alliance" with Prince against narcoterrorism and illegal fishing — a state contract in a country whose six-billion-dollar shrimp industry already spends more than eighty million a year on private security, and whose mass-detention program (hundreds released without charges after a stadium stunt) shadowed the announcement. Scope and value were never disclosed [A: CNA relay; press record].

## 10.7 Mozambique and Somalia: the control cases and the survivor

Mozambique remains the file's controlled experiment: three private waves failed (Wagner's 2019 withdrawal after ambush losses; Dyck Advisory Group's helicopter force terminated in April 2021 days after its finest hour evacuating two hundred civilians from Palma; Paramount's training program) where a national army — Rwanda's — held. The deciding variable was mandate and infantry mass, not skill or airpower; and the postscript writes itself: the contract for the gas project's security ultimately went to the Rwandan ruling party's company (9.1) [A: Amnesty; CNN].

Somalia's PMPF — traced from Prince's concept through Saracen/Sterling to its modern incarnation — seized a hijacked tanker in a forty-eight-hour operation in August 2026, some 4,800 strong, having outlived every corporate parent; and Bancroft Global Development, the Washington nonprofit that assessed and declined the training contract in 2012, continues its African Union training mission with State Department circuitous funding and a documented ambition to protect mining concessions in the Central African Republic — the Washington-sanctioned counter-Wagner model, everything Wagner does with a Georgetown address [S: SWJ; RFI; A: NYT 2011].

## 10.8 The Gaza humanitarian-PMC complex

The Gaza war produced the first theater where an armed contractor layer was inserted between an occupying army and a civilian population as a deliberate policy instrument — inside a humanitarian program.

The **Gaza Humanitarian Foundation** was established in February 2025 as a US- and Swiss-registered nonprofit to replace the UN's distribution system with four militarized aid hubs; a feasibility study had been commissioned from **Orbis Operations** (the private-intelligence firm) in November 2024, funded by undisclosed philanthropy. Its operating record, documented by Responsible Statecraft, NPR, and the United Nations: a burn rate of roughly **$140 million a month** for about three hundred contractors; the lead security contractor **Safe Reach Solutions** (a Wyoming LLC founded by a CIA veteran, owned through a trust benefiting the Chicago private-equity firm McNally Capital) and **UG Solutions** (armed US special-operations veterans; staff included members of the Infidels Motorcycle Club) plus an unnamed Egyptian firm — the first American PMCs in Gaza in decades, operating the Netzarim corridor checkpoint from January 2025. **Boston Consulting Group helped design the operation, set contractor prices, and billed more than $1 million a month** before withdrawing in June 2025 (firing two senior partners for unauthorized work). The State Department approved $30 million in June 2025 with the first-time-grantee audit, congressional consultation, anti-diversion certification, and third-party monitoring all waived; USAID's internal assessment recorded "critical concerns." Donor identity was never disclosed ("a private foundation," said executive chair Johnnie Moore — a former Trump communications adviser; Israel's Kan broadcaster reported Israeli government funding in the hundreds of millions of shekels; Switzerland closed the foundation's Geneva office and opened an investigation).

The casualty record: more than a hundred Palestinians killed at the hubs by mid-2025, including twenty-seven in a single day at Rafah on 3 June 2025; Reuters' February 2026 accounting, from UN data, put the toll in the GHF operations zone at **more than 2,600 killed and 19,000 injured between May and October 2025**. UNRWA's commissioner-general said the operations could amount to war crimes; Amnesty demanded withdrawal; the UN Commission of Inquiry warned principals could bear individual criminal responsibility. After the October 2025 ceasefire the sites paused; Safe Reach was "administratively dissolved" in Wyoming records on 7 October 2025 (the company called it a clerical error); UG hired a Trump-associated lobbying firm and confirmed talks with the **Board of Peace** for the reconstruction phase — the same board whose leaked draft resolution, reported by the Guardian in June 2026, would shield members, military forces, and "non-resident contractors" from arrest, detention, or legal proceedings in Gaza, with public property seizure "free of charge." The reconstruction money, as of September 2026: $17 billion pledged against the Board's own $35.2 billion damage assessment (the World Bank says $53 billion; the UN says $70 billion-plus — the spread is itself a finding), the funds sitting in a J.P. Morgan account without the oversight the World Bank channel would provide; the first building contract went to Arkel International of Louisiana; the International Stabilization Force commanded by Major General Jasper Jeffers III has gathered commitments from Kosovo (signed 7 September 2026), Morocco, Israel, Albania, and Uganda, with Egypt and Jordan to train twelve thousand Gaza police — though the force's envisioned twenty thousand has reportedly scaled down toward twenty-five hundred (Ynet), its commander Jasper Jeffers III in place, an Indonesian deputy designated, and the forty-eight remaining hostages' return part of the deal's architecture. The High Representative, Nickolay Mladenov, told the Security Council on 26 August that Hamas and other groups had agreed to disarm, warned that failure would make a two-state solution "practically impossible," and acknowledged the board's pre-election leverage over Netanyahu was limited; Israel had already told him it would not withdraw from the Yellow Line, reserved the right to strike, and objected to Qatari and Turkish participation in any verification role. And the disarmament mechanics — an "internationally funded buy back and reintegration program" — remain the deal's true fault line, with Hamas conditioning acceptance on withdrawal, a statehood pathway, and retention of small arms [S/A: Reuters; Guardian; PBS; BBC; OHCHR; company records].

Standing a few miles from this apparatus, in the same months: **SOC** guarding the US diplomatic mission in Jerusalem (5.3's record), and the Israeli checkpoint-security firms of Part XI's border layer — Modi'in Ezrachi, T&M, Hashmira — whose guards staff the separation-wall crossings and East Jerusalem compounds under contracts worth tens of millions of shekels, and whose record includes the 2016 Qalandia checkpoint killing of two siblings with no charges filed. The occupation-security complex and the humanitarian-PMC complex share a city and a logic: the transfer of lethal functions to private hands, where review dies [A/B: Who Profits; B'Tselem; OCHA].

## 10.9 The labor pipelines: five mechanisms, five continents

The recruitment economy that feeds every ecosystem sorts into five documented mechanisms, each with its own country ledger:

**1. Broker agencies** — the A4SI model (Part V.5): licensed employment fronts with debt-trapped owners and offshore payroll conduits. **2. State-impersonation scams** — Kenya's "Kazi Majuu" fake government-endorsed work-abroad program, which charged job seekers fees and delivered them to the war; Kenyan security raids rescued twenty-two before departure; WhatsApp reassurance groups ran in Swahili with recruits messaging under duress. **3. Debt-trap recruitment** — Sri Lanka: families sold jewelry and mortgaged houses to pay ~$10,000 in agent fees on promises of $2,100-a-month jobs (thirteen times the average income) plus land; two retired generals were arrested as the alleged masterminds; about 2,000 Sri Lankans fought on both sides, with sixteen confirmed killed by mid-2024; Moscow agreed in June 2024 to stop recruiting; 280 family complaints were on file by mid-2026; the same country deploying three hundred peacekeepers to Haiti's Gang Suppression Force in July 2026 — peacekeeping and mercenarism as twin export products of one underemployed military-labor state. **4. Coercive-migration funnels** — Central Asia: the Ukrainian "I Want to Live" project has identified **12,666 Central Asian contract soldiers** in Russian ranks (Uzbekistan 4,853; Tajikistan 3,407; Kazakhstan 2,389; Kyrgyzstan 1,439; Turkmenistan 578), against Russia's formal 2026 recruitment quota of 18,500 foreigners with Central Asia named the priority; Tajiks specifically targeted after the 2024 Crocus City Hall attack; Russia's Investigative Committee chief claims twenty thousand "new Russian citizens" from the region are at the front. And Cuba: recruiters arrested in Havana even as young men signed for the dream of a passport. **5. Ideological and ultra channels** — the Española hooligans (6.9), the E.N.O.T. youth camps, and the Western mirror-image: the roughly 23,000 American "lone soldiers" in the IDF — same labor economics, opposite legal category, a distinction that is itself a finding.

The documented totals: Ukraine's intelligence service estimates about **28,000 foreigners** brought into Russian units since 2022. Amnesty International's September 2026 briefing — covering Brazil, Colombia, Cuba, Egypt, Sierra Leone, Somalia, and South Africa beyond the better-documented Asian pipelines — adopted the frame this document uses: deceptive, coercive, and exploitative recruitment that in many cases **amounts to human trafficking** [S/A: Amnesty; DW; Militarnyi; timesca].

Two last ledgers complete the labor map: Russian PMCs have recruited **Ukrainian citizens from the occupied territories** for African missions at 240,000 rubles for six-month contracts — occupied-population labor feeding third-country deployments — and North Korea's eleven-thousand-plus troop deployment stands in a category of its own: state-to-state, not mercenary, but the same labor arithmetic at the extreme [A/B: Militarnyi; ISW].

# PART XI — THE CYBER-MERCENARY LAYER

The digital wing of the trade follows the same laws as the kinetic one: the same small group of veterans, the same shell-company migrations, the same government clients, the same accountability gap — and, as of 2025–26, the same absorption of Israeli firms into American balance sheets.

## 11.1 The spyware vendors and their state clients

**NSO Group** (Israel; Pegasus) is the industry standard-bearer: entity-listed by the United States in November 2021, sued by Meta's WhatsApp (a May 2025 verdict found it liable — $444,000 in compensatory damages plus $167 million in punitives, later reduced, with a permanent injunction in October 2025), and in October 2025 **acquired by a US investor group led by Hollywood executive Robert Simonds** — controlling ownership for tens of millions of dollars, with the firm still Israeli-regulated and still entity-listed; the CEO stepped down in January 2026. Along the way NSO bought **Convexum**, an Israeli counter-drone firm founded by Unit 8200 veterans, for $60 million in 2020 — the Pegasus maker owns a drone-defense company [S/A: Treasury; court records; TechCrunch].

**Candiru** (Israel) — also entity-listed in November 2021 — ran a documented renaming treadmill: DF Associates (2017), Grindavik Solutions (2018), Taveta (2019), Saito Tech (2020). **Intellexa** (the Predator consortium, Greek-Cypriot-North Macedonian) and its **Cytrox** unit were entity-listed in July 2023, with founder Tal Dilian individually sanctioned in 2024 — and three Intellexa-linked individuals **removed from the sanctions list in January 2026**, fourteen months later, over the objection of Citizen Lab researchers; Predator activity resumed post-sanctions. **Paragon** — founded in 2019 by former prime minister Ehud Barak and former Unit 8200 commander Ehud Schneorson, marketed as the clean alternative — was acquired in December 2024 by **Red Lattice, a US cyber firm owned by the private-equity fund AE Industrial Partners**, for over $500 million. Its Graphite spyware reached the US government itself: Immigration and Customs Enforcement held a $2 million contract (awarded August 2025; suspended under a Biden-era review, revived under Trump; DHS confirmed use and said the contract was closed out on 20 January 2026). Italy's intelligence service was confirmed as a Graphite user in a scandal that included a journalist, a migrant-rescue organizer, and a priest close to Pope Francis among ninety WhatsApp targets — Paragon terminated the Italian contract in February 2025. Citizen Lab's verdict on the whole architecture: "You Can't Abuse-Proof Mercenary Spyware" — no ownership structure prevents abuse [S/A: as cited throughout].

The zero-day brokers sit one tier down: **Zerodium** (Washington, founded by ex-Vupen founder Chaouki Bekrar) priced iPhone full chains at up to $2.5 million before going "inactive for months... in crisis" in 2024 and never visibly reviving; **Crowdfense** (UAE) and Operation Zero serve the same market with cleaner compliance postures [A/B: Intelligence Online].

## 11.2 The Indian hack-for-hire complex

The labor-market analog of the Nepal/Colombia pipelines runs through Delhi-NCR. **Appin** — founded by Rajat Khare, described by a rival's director as "the godfather for all hackers" — renamed itself Sunkissed Organic Farms and ran subsidiaries including Adaptive Control Security Global; its alumni seeded **BellTroX InfoTech** (whose director Sumit Gupta was indicted in California in 2015; whose "Dark Basin" operation, exposed by Citizen Lab in 2020, targeted thousands of victims across six continents — climate campaigners, short-sellers, prosecutors), **CyberRoot Risk Advisory** (the Azima case — hacking a journalist on behalf of a US private-intelligence firm working for a UAE sovereign fund), and **Rebsec Solutions** (which Google's researchers noted "openly advertises corporate espionage"). The product, per the Bureau of Investigative Journalism's leaked database: litigation manipulation — hacking lawyers and their clients mid-case; more than a thousand American lawyers were targeted. In September 2026 — days before this document was compiled — US lawmakers demanded Entity List action against all three firms [S/A/NA: DOJ; Citizen Lab; BIJ; congressional letters].

## 11.3 The counter-drone software layer

The defensive wing consolidated into American corporates on the same schedule: **Motorola Solutions bought Israel's D-Fend Solutions for $1.5 billion (June 2026)** after a two-week banker-led process; **Axon** bought Dedrone (detection) and **Carbyne** (the Israeli 911 platform, $625 million, November 2025); Wrap Technologies took US distribution of Israeli Frenel imaging; and **Skapion** — founded by the former general manager of Rafael's Iron Dome division — raised $36 million on a deliberate structure: Washington headquarters, Israeli R&D, "the entire company is in the US—it's a US company," purpose-built to sell to the Pentagon. NSO's own Convexum sits in the same stack. The Israeli brain under the American flag is now the standard architecture of the Western counter-drone market [A: company releases; Tectonic Defense].

The Israeli private layer beneath the absorptions, with its client maps: **Skylock/Avnon Group** (founded 2018; deployed in forty-plus countries with named clients — Chile, India, Morocco's Royal Armed Forces, the UAE, Thailand's navy, Ethiopia's capital, Ukraine via Lithuania, Zambia, and US Army/NATO evaluation of its wearable system; 2024: merged into a Tel Aviv Stock Exchange shell at a $19 million valuation after selling subsidiary MyDefense for $130 million) · **ParaZero** (Nasdaq: PRZO; Kfar Saba; the net-capture DefendAir turret — a soft-kill answer for airports and energy sites — with a first US federal government order and NATO-country training-kit contracts, on Israeli defense-entity orders) · **Smart Shooter** (the rifle-mounted fire-control firm with USMC, Navy, Army, and Air Force orders, in US service since SOF rifles were photographed with it in Syria in 2020) · **UVision** (the loitering-munition maker preparing a Nasdaq IPO at a reported $5 billion target). The economics the layer answers: $5.3 million Patriot rounds fired at Iranian loitering munitions forced the US Army's June 2026 Low-Cost Interceptor program (effectors under $1 million); Rheinmetall's Skynex answers at roughly €4,000 per shot; **Epirus's Leonidas microwave system killed a fiber-optic FPV on 13 January 2026 — the first hard-kill answer to jamming-immune drones** — under a $43.5 million Army contract; and Anduril's $640 million in counter-drone awards validated the software-centric newcomers against the primes [A: JPost; company releases; Breaking Defense; Army Technology].

## 11.4 The jihadi-PMC brands

The corporate grammar of the industry is ideology-agnostic. **Malhama Tactical** — founded in 2015–16 by the Uzbek ex-Russian-paratrooper Sukhrob Baltabaev — operated as a fifty-man training company inside Syria's jihadist economy: infantry, sniper, close-quarters, medical courses and PKM accessories sold to the HTS client base, crowdfunded in Bitcoin, run under three successive identities (facial-recognition analysis documented the same man as "Abu Rofiq," "Abu Salman Belarussi," and "Ali Shishani" — surviving, among other things, an airstrike that killed his wife and infant son). Its November 2018 joint operation with HTS — a night raid on a Syrian-Russian position that killed twenty-five, including seven Russians, at zero cost to itself — was the group's masterpiece. It was absorbed into HTS in 2017, re-emerged in December 2024, and dissolved again in the Sharaa government's integration drive; its American fundraiser, Victoria Jacobs, received eighteen years in federal prison in 2023. Its successors — Muhojir Tactical, Yurtugh Tactical, Albanian Tactical — professionalized the product line to include drone warfare. A US expert's 2017 assessment stands as the epigraph: "jihadist mercenary units may soon become a regular feature of global conflict" [S/A: NUS Insight; Foreign Policy; court records; SJAC, ADF].


## 11.5 The private-intelligence registry (the white-collar layer)

Beneath the armed threshold and above journalism sits the intelligence product line of the same officer networks — the advisory layer's (Part I.2) research arm. The Chambers-ranked global roster: **Control Risks, S-RM, Sigma7, Wallbrook, G3** (Band 1); **Kroll** (the original private-intel giant; sold for $1.9 billion in 2004), **Nardello & Co., Quinlan Partners, StoneTurn, Risk Advisory Group, Hakluyt** (Band 2); **K2 Integrity, Templeton Research** (Band 3). The ecosystem list adds **Argen, Merchant International Group, Pinkerton, Stratfor (RANE), Fusion GPS**, and the defunct **Psy-Group** (the social-media-manipulation venture shut after the Mueller-era investigations). The thesis of the literature on this layer (Meier's *Spooked*): "the big money in private spying is made not by exposing the truth but by papering it over or concealing it" — the incentive asymmetry as the Pinkerton pattern's modern form. Adjacent but distinct: the hack-for-hire firms of 11.2 (which execute) and the spyware vendors of 11.1 (which sell tools); this registry *advises* — risk, integrity, investigations — and its ex-spy labor pool is the same reserve army the kinetic layers draw from [B/NA: Chambers; KCS ecosystem].

The emblematic episode: **Beacon Global Strategies' Dataminr affair** — leaked emails showed the firm (founded by the former chief of staff to two national-security agencies, with two ex-CIA directors as senior counselors) arranging conversations between the Twitter-data surveillance firm Dataminr and at least five embassies — Azerbaijan's among them — pitching capabilities "that suggest it could have been used for surveillance," while helping plan the firm's UN General Assembly strategy through an unregistered consultant; Dataminr said it declined to build the capabilities. The ex-arms-bureau founder's firm pitching surveillance to embassies is the advisory layer's quiet product line, documented in its own correspondence [NA-primary: MapLight/DCLeaks].

## 11.6 The unified market picture


The cyber-mercenary layer ends where the kinetic layer began: with a definition problem. The 2023 US executive order restricting government use of risky commercial spyware, the Entity List designations, the WhatsApp verdict, and the congressional letters all treat these firms as a category — yet none of the regimes touches the structural fact documented throughout this part: the same small labor pool (Unit 8200 alumni, CIA veterans, Indian self-taught operators), the same client class (governments first, oligarchs second, law firms third), the same exit (acquisition by a larger American balance sheet), and the same accountability record (one eighteen-year sentence in twenty years, against a market serving seventy-four governments).
